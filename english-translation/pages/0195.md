My speech created a big sensation. I referred to this *Muttamiḻ Viḻā* function to drive home the point that the debate as to whether *ilakkiyam* is derived from *ilaṭciyam* (objective) or from *ilaṅkutal* (shine forth as an example) still continues. We have such a debate in *Tolkāppiyam* also:

> Usage  
> Being the word of the learned,  
> Propriety in poetry  
> Draws on conformity to the usage.

Iḷampūraṇar’s commentary on this verse is that poetic diction should adopt the usage that comes to be the standard from the use of the learned.

Nacciṉārkkiṉiyar, on the other hand, adds the conjunctive particle *um* to *uyarntōr kiḷavi* (the usage of the learned) and says that the learned usage going in accord with the vedic usage becomes the poetic diction.

Here, the word *vaḻakku* (usage) does not refer to any case filed in a court of law! Instead, it refers to usage of words and sentences.

Iḷampūraṇar claims that only the words of learned men can become the standard of usage.

The conjunctive particle of Nacciṉārkkiṉiyar may imply that there exists *iḻintōr kiḷavi* (the diction of the lowly) too, which goes against the norm in *Tolkāppiyam Marapiyal* section that

> Usage is  
> What is in vogue and use  
> Among the learned.

Quoting this in his *Tolkāppiyam* commentary, the great Tamil scholar Veḷḷaivāraṇar points out that *vaḻakku* (usage) is linguistic usage, and reference to vedic usage is out of place.

Another commentator Pērāciriyar agreeing with Iḷampūraṇar says that usage is the use of language by the learned because it is from that the events of popular speech issue.

The use of the lowly, such as

> *eṉṉaṭā tapāykkiṟē* (What man, trying to escape?)  
> *eṉṉammā moṟaikkiṟē* (You woman! Why staring at me?)

can never become norms of usage.
