# Blossom 35 — That Golden Era

In the Hindu temples, the presiding deity has two statues - mūlavar, the stone statue fixed inside sanctum sanctorum and uṟcavar , the statue made of five types of metals. Of them, mūlavar is worshipped in the place of its location, whereas uṟcavar is taken in procession outside the temple, as the representative of mūlavar during festivals.

This practice is seen observed in all the temples of Tamilnāṭu. But the exceptions are the six temples at Tiruvārūr and the adjoining Tirukkuvaḷai, Tirukkōpuravāsal, Tirumaṟaikkāṭu, Tirunākai and Tiruvāymūr, where the mūlavar and uṟcavar statues are not made of stone and metal respectively, and they look alike. The same Tiyākarāja statue is fixed in the sanctum sanctorum and taken in procession also. It is also used in chariot processions. In these shrines, the procession deity has a more pronounced honour of worship.

The devotees and priests carry the statue on their shoulders every year on auspicious days to mark the advent of the festival. They place it on a chariot and celebrate festivals drawing the chariots. At the culmination of the festivals, the statue is reinstated inside the temple.

In the uḻiñai situation of puṟam poetry, Tolkāppiyam Poruḷatikāram speaks of a similar honour accorded by kings to their victorious swords.

The uḻiñai puṟam situation deals with the siege of an enemy fort by a king and the defence of the fort by the king remaining within. The siege is resorted to when the authority of the attacking king is defied by the other.
