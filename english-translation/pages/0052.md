# Blossom 9 — Cōḻaṉ · continued

“In that case, I know a little of literature, as I happen to be a retired Tamil teacher. I would ask you a question. Would you answer?”

“Please do ask, grandmother. I will answer!”

“Suppose I say that Cōḻaṉ would come (*varum*) to take me, is Cōḻaṉ here human or non-human class?”

“Grandmother, if Cōḻaṉ referred to by you happens to be your son or grandson, it is human! In case Cōḻaṉ points to the bus, it is non-human class.”

“Son, how do you say so? What is its grammatical explanation?”

“Grandmother, look! *Tolkāppiyam*’s *Eḻuttatikāram* is crystal clear in this regard:

> There is non-change coalescence too  
> Where  
> Nouns that designate  
> Both the human and non-human generic classes  
> Are the preceding words.

The noun that takes both human and non-human classes is called a common noun. That is how my explanation holds good. The human masculine Cōḻaṉ normally becomes non-human.”

“Dear grandson! You are more talented than Lord Murukaṉ himself who proved his mettle before Avvai, in those days. While he demonstrated his Tamil proficiency only with regard to a fruit, you have revealed your talent in age-old Tamil grammar. May you flourish in your life! May you flourish in your life!”

The Cōḻaṉ came (*vantuviṭṭāṉ*) during the shower of greetings. No, no, it came (*vantuviṭṭatu*). The old lady planted a kiss upon the forehead of the youth and boarded the bus with his assistance.

***

> There is non-change coalescence too  
> Where  
> Nouns that designate  
> Both the human and non-human generic classes  
> Are the preceding words.
>
> (*Eḻuttatikāram : Tokaimarapu*, verse 13)
