Such an approach should inform our study of ancient works.

A piece of literature which reflects the conditions of that age need not be considered as containing norms of behaviour applicable for today.

Instead, we should understand the basic idea the ancient works attempt to project. We should also be clear about the extent to which it should be taken and on what basis. We must see how these ideas and attitudes bear on the current realities.

If we were to undo the foundations of the past in our pursuit of newness, then in our homes the pictures of our grandparents and parents and the articles used by them along with the other traces of the yesteryears would have to be jettisoned as useless objects.

When ancient literature projects the improbable as real, it may be left aside and need not be believed in. In the modern age, the trains which are powered by electricity or for that matter aeroplanes which fly with the assistance of technology have wheels. The chariots of kings of yore had also run on wheels which might have been different in size or shape. Even today, bullock carts with wheels can be seen in small hamlets, just as we find buses and cars running on wheels.

In the same manner, though significant changes have surfaced in culture, ethos and lifestyle, the fundamentals remain unchanged.

At this juncture of this book when I have already penned sixty chapters, I must mention that there is a need for us to look back and then proceed further.

*Tolkāppiyam, Poruḷiyal* speaks of certain characteristics specific to women, which, if we look at objectively, deserve to be appreciated rather than condemned.

> Temperance, fulness of bearing, uprightness,  
> Facility of the tongue, discrimination and discernment  
> Are traits marked in women.

(*Poruḷatikāram : Poruḷiyal*, verse 15)
