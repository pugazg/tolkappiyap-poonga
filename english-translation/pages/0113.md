# Blossom 34 — Outsmarting the Smartest · continued

The columns of opposing soldiers appear like the winged ants from groves to make one wonder: Is the country too small for the army? Or is the army too large for the country? They march on as if they are unbridled horses, and with eyes burning like tigers’. “Let fire engulf the way we pass through! Let it spread in all directions! And let it reduce the flower garden-like fields to ashes.”

They say, “Our king has earned undeclining fame by his valour”. They praise the king of their country for his chivalry of liberally donating the prize articles in the form of battle-arms. The soldiers compose songs and sing them to the accompaniment of stringed instruments. “Victory greets our king in all the places, whereas other kings are doomed to destruction.” They would even say it was their king who added lustrous embellishment to the word ‘victory’ itself. So go their unabashed hyperboles of flatteries.

Like the mighty dam, which stops the overflowing waters of a river threatening to breech the banks, due to unabated rain marking the peak of the rainy season, there is also the one who singlehandedly defends his land against the aggression of the seemingly never-ending columns of invading army, out to conquer the land. He shatters their overvaulting avarice and leaves them dejected. The conqueror is at the same time not bloated over the ruin of the vanquished. He believes in the cultured doctrine of civility that it is always better to regard victory and defeat in the same manner. Therefore, he provides aid and food to those injured in the war. He exempts the vanquished from paying tribute and stands with no desire to seek vengeance. He remains tall as an epitome of marvellous fame.

The greedy king, who sought to invade, beats a hasty retreat to his country by declaring that he doesn’t need an enlarged kingdom, instead whatever he is left with is good enough.

***

> The *vañci puram* theme  
> Ramifies into thirteen substrands of worthy note:  
> The clamour that marks the army’s march;  
> Setting the foemen’s land on fire;  
> The radiant glory that attends the feats of prowess;  
> The acts of liberality extended by the monarch;  
> The acts of valour  
> With which the enemy is charged at close quarters  
> And is sent to his doom;  
> The boast of the warriors  
> As recipients of the ruler’s gifts and honours;
