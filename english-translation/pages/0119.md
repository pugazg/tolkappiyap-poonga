> It is in particular  
> The theme of undoing the might of an invading monarch  
> Burning with an overweening pride in his prowess.
>
> (*Poruḷatikāram : Puṟattiṇai iyal*, verse 15)

> The life of the warrior in action departing,  
> With his body having been pierced thick and fast  
> By the rain of arrows and spears of foemen,  
> There is the wondrous spectacle of the man in two pieces -  
> The body standing erect without falling on the ground  
> In the manner of the leech cut into parts staying alive.
>
> (*Poruḷatikāram : Puṟattiṇai iyal*, verse 16)

Men on foot, elephants and cavalry march on to the field, sending shivers down the spine of the enemy. The drums are played on both the sides to herald the start of the war. One of them sounds like the thunder of the summer season, while the other sounds like the storm, crossing through the sea, causing a great upheaval.

The swords blaze through the warfield; the spears are launched from the mighty hands. The arrows, ripping through the air, pierce the stomach of a few lethargic men, announcing that a timid person has no place in the field of war. A few exceptional instances surface in which cowards, whose bodies shiver like strings of a musical instrument, running through in fear are identified and sidelined.

A soldier fighting at a distance witnesses his king, dear to him like his own life, hemmed in by enemy soldiers and commanders like columns of fish. “What, is my king in danger?” he reacts with blood-shot eyes and with hands clutching on to his weapon, he pounces there, much like an injured tiger or rather like a stamped snake.

On both the sides, soldiers form protective rings around their kings and safeguard them. The army commander moves around the columns of his army. In the event of any unexpected setback, he remains unfazed quite like a buffalo, only to single-handedly take on the might of the formidable army of the foe. The buffalo can bear anything. It is given such an honour in *puṟam* literature.
