# Blossom 88 — The Sources of Similitude · continued

The poet lauds the unity and friendship between the two kings.

Just as the two kings were seated together in the court, music and dance combined to fill the city with joy and festivities.

This simile has excellence as its basis.

Here excellence is not a natural trait, but something added.

The *nalam* (loveliness) mentioned in the verse refers to a natural aspect arising from a thing. It is like the water spurting naturally while a well is dug. Excellence, on the other hand, is like making a pond and digging canals from the river to facilitate flow of water into it.

The excellence is the basis for the simile of music and dance going together becoming the standard for Cōḻaṉ Karikālaṉ and Pāṇṭiyaṉ Peruvaḻuti seated together in the court.

The residents of Maruvūrppākkam - traders, yavaṉars, weavers of silk, cotton and woollen clothes, goldsmiths, flower vendors, sculptors, sailors, physicians, brahmins, officials of the palace, bards, songsters - added to the beauty of the city.

Already, the Grand Arch which the king of Avanti constructed as a gift to Karikāl Cōḻaṉ, the Auditorium of Pearls, a present from the monarch of Vajra, and the Pavilion, a tribute from the ruler of Maghada added lustre to the city of Pūmpukār rendering it a picture gallery and a treasure house of sculpture.

This simile then serves as a source of loveliness.

In another chapter of the same historical novel, Ceḷiyaṉ, a character, wonders at a beautiful woman named Juno in the city of Rome:

> Oh! Seeing this Juno, I feel as if my Tāmarai has descended on the city of Rome after having flown winged angels-like by a boon from God.

This imagination flashing across the mind of Ceḷiyaṉ reflects the deep affection he has for his love residing in Tamiḻ Nāṭu. Therefore, the source of this simile is love.

In the same novel, a poem about one of the important characters, Karikāl Cōḻaṉ, written by Kaṭiyalūr Uruttiraṅkaṇṇanār finds a mention:

> The spear of Karikāl Tirumāvaḷavaṉ hurled at his foes is ferocious;  
> But even more horrible is the forest ventured into by the lovers.  
> But the shoulder of the lady seemed more comforting than Karikālaṉ’s sceptre.

Here, the horrible forest functions as the simile for the ferocity of Karikālaṉ’s spear. Therefore, valour is the basis for this simile.

Obviously then, a simile arises primarily on the bases of excellence, loveliness, love and might.
