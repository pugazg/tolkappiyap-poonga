# Blossom 68 — Semantic Shift

Our forefathers advise us to rein in anger (*ciṉam*), as anger spoils relationships, destroys unity, and mars friendship. That is why anger is vicious. It should be avoided. Anger cannot be accepted in any form. But there are instances where the word ‘anger’ is used in a positive sense.

For example, when an army of brave youth rises to defend the Tamil language, we go in for expressions like the one below:

> Look at the upsurge of the angry tigers  
> That march up  
> Beating the drum of racial honour!

Though the emotion of anger is generally not accepted, the term attains a significant status in this context.

*Pētaimai* can be interpreted as ignorance. The dictionary defines *pētai* as a young woman. There are women who witness with glee the plight of a woman ‘possessed’ being thrashed by exorcists.

While denouncing such women as *pētai*, we are not using the term in any positive sense.

But if an old man who marries a young woman dies within six months of the wedding, we take pity on the young widow and call her a poor, unfortunate girl. When we use the word *pētai* for the young widow, the meaning changes. The word here does not denote folly but her pathetic condition as a young widow. Therefore in this context, *pētaimai* gains in stature as a special term referring to pathos.
