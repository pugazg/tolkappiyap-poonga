Paracurāmaṉ and Rāmaṉ were contemporaries.  
Akattiyar belonged to the period of Rāmaṉ.  
Tolkāppiyar too belonged to the same period.

What is strange here is that Rāma’s age is fixed before 2000 BC. Pāṇini’s period was 350 BC. Had Tolkāppiyar lived after Pāṇini, Paṉampāraṉār in his prologue to Tolkāppiyam might not have described Tolkāppiyar as the one versed in Aintiram. He would have called him versed in Pāṇini’s treatise. As such, it is obvious that Tolkāppiyam preceded Pāṇini’s work.

Now, let’s come to the Tolkāppiyam verse referred to above. It does not speak of whether Tolkāppiyam is a primary text or not. On the other hand, it discusses the nature of secondary works. A secondary work implies that there must be a primary work. The verse in question just outlines the what and how of a primary work.

But some of our scholar-commentators tend to give the impression that the author of a primary work is God.

Not just that. Shouldn’t we realize the wily misdirection behind such assertions like Akattiyam is the primary work of the ancient era, and Tolkāppiyam is based on this work.

As I have shown earlier, we must ignore such things as to whether Tolkāppiyam is a primary work and whether the primary work was authored by God. And we must treat this and the following verses in Tolkāppiyam as describing primary and secondary works.

The verse

> viṉaiyiṉ nīṅki viḷaṅkiya aṟiviṉ  
> muṉaivaṉ kaṇṭatu mutal nūlākum

must read as

> viḷaṅkiya aṟiviṉ muṉaivaṉ kaṇṭatu  
> viṉaiyiṉ nīṅki mutal nūlākum

which means that men of consummate wisdom write what are called primary works, works that are faultless and that precede secondary works.

Verses 3 and 4 speak of the four kinds of secondary works: synthesis, elaboration, both synthesis and elaboration, and translation.
